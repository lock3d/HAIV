About the model

HAIV runs the model from Organizing Intelligence (Imas, Koh and Legg, Google DeepMind, 2026). People and AI copies sort themselves into competing firms; what you see on the stage is the industry that maximises total surplus, which the paper shows is the equilibrium (Theorem 1).

Production

An agent is a person or an AI copy. Every agent has two task skills and a coordination capability. A firm of size m with mean skills (y1, y2) and mean coordination z produces

F = L(y1, y2) × m (1+m)^(z−1) e^(φ m / (1+m)), with L = ((y1^ρ + y2^ρ)/2)^(1/ρ).

Competence L shifts output per head up or down. Coordination z bends the scale curve: below one, output per head peaks at the preferred size m* = φ/(1−z) − 1 (the Coasean ceiling); above one it rises without limit, and a firm grows until it is as large as its market.

People and AI

People are in fixed supply, so scarce capabilities earn a premium over the outside option. AI copies can be rented at the compute cost, so identical copies never earn that premium. Demand is linear: the price falls from the top willingness to pay to zero at the market’s size.

How it is solved

For a given output, the cheapest industry is a linear programme over firm recipes (a mix of roles and a size), exactly as in the paper’s activity analysis. Wages and the compute rent are its shadow values; output is then chosen to maximise surplus. The solver reproduces the figures printed in the paper, including Table 4 and the displacement thresholds.

Organisation rules

The rules restrict which firms may exist: everyone solo, a fixed or capped size, one organisation, no human–AI mixing, no cross-role teams, a minimum human share. The solver then finds the best industry inside the rule, so the gap to the free equilibrium is the cost of that structure. The structure league in the readouts runs all of them side by side.

Reading the stage

Every mark is one person or one AI copy; every hexagon is one firm. Firm types stand in a row from human-only on the left to AI-only on the right. People no firm would pay more than the outside option, and copies that are not rented, wait in the pools underneath. When you change something, each agent keeps its seat if it can and travels only if it has to.

What is added to the paper

  • Fleet limit. The paper lets AI be copied without limit. Here the fleet is capped at up to 100,000; when the cap binds, compute earns a rent.
  • Jaggedness and individual variation. Jaggedness scales how far each role’s two skills sit from their average. Individual variation spreads people around their role; their pay follows the paper’s affine bid schedule (Theorem 2) and the least valuable are the first outside firms.
  • Whole firms. The paper allows fractional firm counts. Numbers on this page use those; the stage rounds them to whole firms and whole people.
  • Trust and Intercompany. Both off unless you switch them on; see below.

Trust

The paper has no trust in it: people and AI copies work together without friction. With the switch on, trust is the share of AI decisions people act on without checking, from 0 to 1. What the AI warrants is how often its call beats the average person’s, q = L_AI / (L_AI + L_people), taken from their competence.

Only the gap between trust and q matters, and only where people and AI work together. In a firm with human share h, a share x = 2h(1−h) of working pairs joins a person and a copy. Then:

  • Speed. More trust means less back-and-forth, so mixed teams coordinate faster: z + 0.5 × x × gap. Less trust slows them.
  • Errors. Trust above what the AI warrants lets its mistakes through: L × (1 − 1.5 × x × gap).
  • Oversight. Trust below it keeps people in the loop: every firm must be at least 0.5 × |gap| human, so no firm runs on AI alone.

At a gap of zero all three vanish and the result is the paper’s. Firm sizes, who works with whom, AI use and wages then follow from the solver as before. The three strengths are this sandbox’s assumptions, not estimates from data.

Intercompany

In the paper firms do not work together. With the switch on, firms link into networks, and links work like coordination inside a firm, one level up: firms are to a network what agents are to a firm. A firm with n partner firms multiplies its output per agent by

H = (1+n)^(ζ−1) × e^(ψ n / (1+n)),

the paper’s scale curve again. ψ is what linking adds and ζ is how well coordination carries across firm boundaries, so a network has a preferred number of partners, n* = ψ/(1−ζ) − 1, and none at all when ψ is below 1−ζ. The three kinds of link each set one part of it:

  • Goods and inventory transfers. Dividing work across firms: a share of the gain from dividing work inside one, ψ = goods × φ.
  • IP licensing. What a firm’s best people know is shared across its network. Each task skill moves from the firm’s average toward its skill-weighted average (each person counted by how good they are at the task). The competence this adds, in logs, is added to ψ. Firms whose people all have the same skills gain nothing.
  • Shared services. Centralised support carries a firm’s own coordination across its boundary: ζ = services × z.

A network cannot have more firms than its market or the headcount allows. Where headcount is short, firms choose their size and their partners together; where the market is, a firm keeps its size and has fewer partners. With Trust on as well, a mixed firm’s links inherit its slower or faster coordination. The mapping of the three links onto the curve is this sandbox’s assumption, not an estimate from data.

Two terms

Concentration is the Herfindahl index (HHI): near 0 when output is spread over many firms, 1 when a single firm makes all of it. A wage is what one more person in a role would add to total surplus; economists call this a shadow value.

Reading it with care

These are outcomes of a stylised model, not forecasts. Units are those of the paper’s example: one AI copy costs one unit of output value per period.
